The Last Ferry to Gull Point Takes the Bread With It

By Ines Carrow · Photographs by Dario Welk · September 17, 2026

The first boat to Gull Point leaves the town dock at 4:50 in the morning, and for forty years it has carried the island's bread. Not most of it. All of it. There is no oven on Gull Point big enough to feed two hundred people, and nobody has wanted to build one while the Pell family bakery on Front Street keeps loading its racks onto the deck before dawn.

Nora Pell, who runs the bakery with her brother, says the arrangement started as a favor. The island store asked for a dozen loaves one winter when the road to the mainland mill washed out. The dozen became forty, then a standing order, then a line in the ferry company's timetable: bread and mail, 4:50.

Weather days

We rode along for a week in late August. Four mornings were glass. On the fifth, a southwest wind stacked the sound into short, steep chop, and the deckhand lashed the racks to the rail with the same care he gave the fuel drums. The captain slowed to half speed. The loaves arrived dry, and the store had them on the shelf by six.

"People think the hard part is the baking," Nora says. "The hard part is the forty minutes on the water. You can't re-proof a loaf that's been soaked."

What the island buys

The order changes with the season. Summer means sandwich loaves for the day-trippers and a tray of cinnamon twists the store sells out of by nine. Winter is heavier: rye, a dense oat loaf the fishermen take out on the boats, and on Fridays a braided loaf that the island's two churches both claim as tradition.

The ferry company renews its contract every spring. Nobody on either side of the sound expects it not to.
