Who Owns a Family Recipe?

By Tobiah Lusk · September 10, 2026

When Lia and Marta Ferreira opened their restaurant on the harbor last spring, the soup on the first page of the menu was their grandmother's caldo verde: kale cut into threads, potatoes cooked down until they give up, a slice of smoked sausage floating in each bowl. Within a month a cousin had written to them, politely and then less politely, to say the recipe was not theirs to sell.

The cousin's argument was simple. The soup had been made at every family gathering for sixty years. Everyone had stood at the pot. Selling it at eleven dollars a bowl, she wrote, turned a thing the family shared into a thing two members of it owned.

Measured in cups

The sisters' answer was also simple. Their grandmother had never written the recipe down. What went on the menu was the version they had rebuilt over three years from memory, phone calls and a great many failed pots, and it was not quite the same soup. "Grandma used whatever greens were cheap," Marta says. "We use lacinato because it holds up on a steam table."

Recipes, as it happens, are hard to own in any legal sense; a list of ingredients is generally treated as a fact rather than a creative work. But the Ferreiras' dispute was never really about law. It was about who gets to speak for the dead.

A truce, for now

The family has settled on something like a truce. The menu now says our grandmother's caldo verde, as we remember it, and on the first Sunday of every month the soup is free to anyone who walks in and says they are a Ferreira. So far, the sisters say, forty-one people have.
