Salt Season

By Maren Hask · Photographs by the author · September 3, 2026

Bellhaven had nine salt works in 1880. It has one now, a row of shallow timber pans on the east marsh run by Aurel Dunne and whoever he can persuade to help. From June until the end of August he floods the pans with seawater, lets the sun and wind do most of the work, and rakes the crust that forms into low white ridges.

The rake he uses was his great-uncle's. Its handle has been replaced twice; the head has not. "It's the right weight," he says, which is the kind of answer you get from Aurel about most things.

Wind, then patience

Good salt weather is not hot weather. It is dry, steady wind from the northwest, which pulls moisture off the brine faster than sunshine alone. A still, humid week can stall a pan completely. A single afternoon thunderstorm can dissolve a week's crust back into the sea, and Aurel has learned to read the sky over the point the way a fisherman reads the tide table.

In a good season the works produces about two tons. Most of it goes to restaurants along the coast and to a spice merchant two towns over who blends it with dried kelp. A small amount is sold from a shed at the end of the marsh road on Saturday mornings, weighed out on a brass scale into paper bags.

The last week

We spent the final week of the season with him in August. On the last day he raked the pans clean, opened the sluices, and let the marsh take them back until June.
